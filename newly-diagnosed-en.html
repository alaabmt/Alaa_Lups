Your first steps after a lupus diagnosis

Four steps to help you understand your diagnosis and treatment, prepare for appointments, and navigate everyday life. Start with any step; there is no required order.

Step 1 of 4

What does my lupus diagnosis mean?

Lupus is a long-term autoimmune condition that can cause inflammation in different parts of the body. Its effects differ from person to person. Being diagnosed does not mean you will experience every possible complication. Start by understanding your own situation.

Optional detail: open any question using (+) without leaving this page.

What is lupus, and how can it affect my body?

Lupus involves a change in how the immune system works and can lead to inflammation affecting joints, skin, blood, kidneys, or other organs. Not everyone has the same symptoms. The fact that an organ can be affected does not mean it is affected in your case. Your clinician's evaluation identifies what matters for you.

How did my healthcare team reach the diagnosis?

Clinicians consider symptoms, medical history, examination, and test results together. One test, such as an antinuclear antibody (ANA) result, is generally not sufficient on its own to diagnose lupus. Ask which findings support your diagnosis.

Why might I need blood and urine tests?

Tests can help assess blood cells, kidney function, and other findings your clinician considers relevant. Important changes sometimes appear in testing before symptoms are noticeable, so follow-up may matter even when you feel well. Review results with your care team rather than interpreting a single number alone.

Will my lupus be like someone else's?

Not necessarily. Symptoms, organs affected, the course of illness, and treatment responses differ. Other people's experiences can prompt useful questions, but they cannot predict what will happen in your case.

Which three questions should I ask first?

  • Which symptoms or organs are affected by lupus in my case so far?
  • What further tests or evaluations do I need?
  • What is our treatment and follow-up plan, and when should I contact the team before my next visit?

What can wait?

You do not need to become a lupus expert this week.

You can learn detailed antibody names, uncommon complications and specialist terminology later if they become relevant. For now, prioritize your own disease pattern, treatment purpose, follow-up plan, safety instructions and daily-life priorities.

Key takeawayLupus differs between people. Ask what is affecting you and what needs follow-up, rather than assuming future complications.

Step 2 of 4

How do I understand treatment and follow-up?

You do not need to memorize every medicine or test. Focus on why each treatment is prescribed, what your care team is monitoring, and which instructions remain unclear. Do not change treatment on your own.

Step 4 of 4

How can I navigate everyday life with lupus?

Your health plan is more than medicines and tests. Sleep, energy, work, family life, and relationships matter too. Choose a priority and raise it with your healthcare team.

What matters to me beyond the diagnosis?

A new diagnosis can quickly make every conversation about tests and medicines. Your work or study, family responsibilities, sleep, energy, relationships, plans for pregnancy, movement and emotional wellbeing also matter.

Choose one life priority you want your healthcare team to understand. It can become part of the care conversation rather than something you manage silently.

You are not expected to predict every flare or understand every laboratory result. Your role is to participate, notice, ask and follow the plan you have agreed with your healthcare team.

More information: when to contact your care team
Safety

Know who to contact before you need them.

Ask your healthcare team which changes should prompt contact before the next appointment and what to do outside clinic hours. Severe or rapidly worsening symptoms require medical assessment rather than website self-assessment.

Do not assume every new symptom is lupus and do not change treatment on your own because you think you are having a flare.
