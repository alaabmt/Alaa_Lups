Juzi: What the empty boxes sayAge 30 · 8 years with lupus
  • Age 30
  • 8 years with lupus
  • Photovoice study, 8 Chinese women

No one keeps empty medicine boxes.

When the medicine is gone, the box has no more meaning. We throw it away, like everything we no longer need.

But Juzi did something different. She collected them.

Juzi is 30. She has lived with lupus for eight years, a companion she did not choose. She looked at what treatment had left behind: empty boxes, empty bottles, used injection materials. She did not throw them away. She put them together in one picture.

Many women in the study said that treatment is work that repeats again and again. It is tiring, and it is hard to explain to other people. It is effort without witnesses. Nobody applauds it, and nobody sees it.

Effort that nobody sees is like a price paid every day without a receipt. It is paid, and then nothing remains to show it.

The boxes could have been a sign of weakness. Every day they could have said that her body needs help to go on, and that medicine is a crutch she cannot put down.

But Juzi read them in another way. She said she likes collecting them. Then she gave them a name:

“…proof that I’ve been persisting and fighting against lupus.”

Here the story changes. The price that was paid without a receipt now has receipts, and they fill the picture. Every box is a receipt. Every bottle is a receipt. Every injection tool is a receipt for a day that was done and not forgotten. The researchers saw that her collage made the hidden work of treatment tangible and countable.

An illness that cannot be seen is often asked for proof. It is as if Juzi did not wait to be asked. She collected her proof with her own hands.

Maybe the picture was not only for her. Maybe it was a silent message to people who cannot see: this is what it takes to keep going.

Eight years. Many empty boxes. And one woman who was not emptied.

A question for you: When you look at your own treatment routine, what does it show about your effort? You can bring your answer to your next visit.

Prepare for your visit →

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Taking your medicine as planned matters in lupus. If the routine is hard or you miss many doses, tell your care team. They may be able to make the schedule simpler. Do not stop or change a medicine on your own. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

Jia: “Endless needles”Age 32 · 5 years with lupus
  • Age 32
  • 5 years with lupus

A hand and forearm, with thin needles in the skin. There is no face in the picture, no cry, no tear. Yet everything in it speaks.

Jia is 32 and has lived with lupus for five years. She did not get the rashes that people fear. But the pain in her joints travels. It is like a heavy guest: today it stays in one place, tomorrow in another. It does not ask and it does not warn. She says:

“I never know which part will hurt when I wake up the next day.”

The worst thing about pain with no address is that you cannot get ready for it. You wake up and ask your body: where today? And it does not answer. Her treatment is not only a pill. It is needle therapy, medicine and patches. After work, when other people go out to have fun, she goes alone to the hospital to get a little relief. It is as if the evening, the first thing a person owns in a day, became a fixed appointment to repair the body.

But the picture did something pain alone cannot do: it made the pain visible. The researchers read the open, still hand and the active needles as a loss of control. They said repeated treatment turned her free time into time for fixing the body, and that the photo turned her symptoms into a story others can share. A private pain became words that others could hear.

If your pain travels like hers, you may know that the hardest part is not how strong it is, but not knowing where it will be. And explaining to others a pain that has no place to point to. You are not alone in this.

A small step that may help: write one line each morning. Where is the pain today, how strong is it, what could you do? Then the unknown becomes a page you can show your doctor. This is our suggestion, not medical advice.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Needle therapy (acupuncture) is comforting for some people, but it is not a proven treatment for lupus and it does not replace it. If a joint becomes swollen or red, or you have a fever, contact your care team. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

Duoduo: “Sixty percent”Age 25 · 2 years with lupus
  • Age 25
  • 2 years with lupus

At the rubbish station, where things go when they are no longer useful, Duoduo stood and photographed used batteries. Small, thrown away, ignored. It could have been a picture with no meaning. But she said: this is me.

Duoduo is 25 and has lived with lupus for two years. She says she can lie down for 20 hours out of 24. This is not laziness. Laziness is a choice, and what she describes is not her choice. She says:

“…a battery that can never be fully charged, one with a maximum capacity of only sixty percent.”

Twenty hours means only four hours are left for everything a person needs to feel present: to eat, to talk, to do something. And maybe the heaviest part is that nobody sees it. Someone who looks at her cannot know how much energy it took to arrive at this moment.

Look at what she did with that sentence. She did not say “I am tired.” Everyone says that word, and people answer it with advice to sleep. It is as if she chose a number. A number cannot be argued with: sixty. Not ninety-five, not a hundred.

The researchers saw more than a low battery that one hour fills. They saw tiredness that lowers ability and threatens her sense of worth. In their reading, “sixty percent” means her top level itself has changed, not that she had one bad day.

If you live something close to this, you may know what we mean: your days are measured by a scale that nobody else sees, and you hear “just rest a little” as if rest fixes everything. You are not alone.

Something in Duoduo’s work is our own comment, not the article’s: naming your limit honestly is a quiet kind of courage, and saying the number helps your doctor hear you. A small step may be to tell your doctor the number you live with, not only the word “tired”. This is not medical advice.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Severe tiredness has many causes that can be checked: lupus activity, low blood count (anemia: too little of the substance that carries oxygen in the blood), the thyroid, sleep and mood. Do not assume it is your final limit. Tell your doctor clearly. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

Luo-yi: “My gear”Age 48 · 14 years of illness
  • Age 48
  • 14 years of illness
  • Lupus nephritis (kidney lupus)

Some people leave home with their keys and their phone. Luo-yi leaves with her gear: a wig, masks and sun protection, arranged like a soldier’s kit before going out.

Luo-yi is 48. Her illness has lasted 14 years, including lupus nephritis, which is lupus in the kidneys. Chemotherapy took all her hair. Then long-term steroids (cortisone-type medicines that reduce inflammation) and kidney disease brought other effects. She was advised to limit make-up, and some changes in the face caused by steroids are hard to hide.

A body that changes without asking tires a person twice: once by the illness, and once by the looks of others. Maybe the hardest part is not what was lost, but standing in front of the mirror each morning and deciding again how to meet the world. She says about her gear:

“Good wigs matter a lot. If they look natural, I do not feel inferior. Cheaper ones are recognizable.”

Some may read the gear harshly: a mask that hides, a fake. But her words change the reading: she is not hiding to cheat. She is hiding so she does not feel less than others. The researchers saw it too: these things stand between a changed body and the eyes of others. They make her feel less exposed and more able to join ordinary social life, although they cannot hide the illness completely.

If you have something like this that helps you go out, you may know how much feeling “like everyone” can cost: time, money and effort, each time. That does not mean you are exaggerating.

What you do is not weakness or show. It is care for your right to walk in the street comfortably. Ask yourself: what small thing helps me go out comfortably? And have I told my care team? They may know how to help with sun, skin and make-up. This is not medical advice.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Do not stop steroids or lower the dose on your own because of how you look or the side effects. Stopping suddenly can be dangerous. Talk to your doctor, because there may be other options. Protection from the sun is important in lupus. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

Xiao-zeng: “My wheelchair”Age 26 · 9 years of illness
  • Age 26
  • 9 years of illness
  • Bone damage in the hip from steroids

In this picture there is no face and no body. Only a part of a wheelchair, and that is all.

Xiao-zeng is 26. Her illness has lasted nine years. Steroids (cortisone-type medicines that reduce inflammation) damaged the bone at the top of her thigh. This is called femoral head necrosis: part of the bone dies because blood cannot reach it well. When she was asked to photograph her illness, she did not photograph herself. She photographed a part of her wheelchair. Perhaps she did this because the chair says what she does not need to say.

As the article says, she hardly goes out to socialize, because her father carries her up and down the stairs. Every outing starts long before the door: with counting the steps, who will carry her, and how it will look in other people’s eyes.

The researchers compared her with another woman in the study. Things that can be put on and taken off can be hidden. A wheelchair cannot. It is illness made visible to every passer-by. They said she goes out and joins social life less, not only because of pain, but to avoid stares and pity. Maybe pity was heavier than the stairs.

If you go out less, like her, you are not alone, and it is not weak will. Maybe ask yourself what really stops you: your body, your home, or other people’s looks? Each one has a different answer.

A small step: ask your care team what support is available for movement, and tell one person you trust what weighs on you most. This is not medical advice. And a father who carries his daughter up the stairs tells us that she has someone who loves her enough to get tired for her.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Bone damage linked to steroids often starts with pain in the hip, thigh or groin. Tell your care team early about any new pain there. Physiotherapy and rehabilitation can also help with movement. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

Xiao-yi: “A healthy meal”Age 23 · More than 1 year with lupus
  • Age 23
  • More than 1 year with lupus

She was asked to photograph her illness. She photographed food: a plate prepared with care. It looks nothing like illness, and yet it looks like it in every way.

Xiao-yi is 23 and was diagnosed more than a year ago. She did not photograph medicine at all. The reason, as she tells it, is that her doctor said being happy and eating well matter for this illness. She took his words seriously and changed her routine. She said:

“So I try to eat healthily every day.”

After a diagnosis, food becomes a list of banned things for many people, and the table becomes a place of loss. Maybe the hardest part is that illness enters even the simplest pleasure: sitting down to a plate and eating.

But the researchers saw something else in her photo: choosing and preparing, a daily act of will and self-care, not just following instructions. It is as if she found a door to take care of herself, instead of standing long in front of the door of loss. Maybe the most beautiful part of the picture is that it has no medicine: illness is not everything in her life, even if it is present in every decision.

If food is a list of bans for you, you may know how heavy that is, and you are not alone. It may help to ask what is really forbidden and what you only think is forbidden.

The small step here is not a diet. Choose one thing you do for yourself every day that feels like your own choice: a meal you make, a short walk, a quiet cup of tea. This is not medical or diet advice.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Good food supports health, but it does not treat lupus and does not replace medicine. If your illness gets worse, it is not your food’s fault. Some patients need real food limits (for example with kidney disease, blood pressure or sugar when taking steroids (cortisone-type medicines)), so ask your care team. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

Lucy: “My mother’s hands”Age 26 · 3 years with lupus
  • Age 26
  • 3 years with lupus

In the picture there are two hands. No face, no words, and no need for words.

Lucy is 26 and has lived with lupus for three years. She was in hospital for a short stay for an infusion (medicine given through a vein). When it ended, she took a photo of two hands holding her hand and pressing it. The hands are her mother’s. She says:

“After diagnosis, my family’s support became really important.”

Her mother did not only talk. She stayed with her in the hospital, fetched medicine, and went with her to the lab. Lucy said her mother’s company helped her find the belief to keep living. A long illness does not only weigh on the body. Sometimes it weighs on the belief that tomorrow is worth it.

On the other side of the picture there is a mother who said her heart was breaking because she could not take the pain from her daughter. It is as if the hands said, without words: I am here, even if I cannot take anything from you. The researchers saw that this support is not pity from far away. It is physical presence, practical help and emotional steadiness, and it helped her continue and eased the isolation.

If someone holds your hand on the heavy days, you may know that what they do is not a small duty. And if no one does, you are not alone, and it does not mean you do not deserve a hand to hold.

A small step: tell the people who love you how they can really help, because many do not know what to offer. And say thank you, even with one word. If you need more support, ask your care team about services for patients and families. This is not medical advice.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Someone who cares for a patient needs support too. If treatment visits or hospital stays feel heavy for you or your family, tell your care team. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

Xiao-mian: “I hate you, ANA!”Age 27 · 1.5 years with lupus
  • Age 27
  • 1.5 years with lupus

A printed page, numbers, and a line with a result. Xiao-mian could have photographed many things about her illness. She chose this.

Xiao-mian is 27 and has lived with lupus for a year and a half. She photographed a lab report: the antinuclear antibody (ANA) test. ANA are antibodies that doctors look for in autoimmune diseases. She named her picture “I Hate You, ANA!”. It is a title that speaks not to an illness, but to a piece of paper. The article gives no direct quote from her, so we use only what the researchers wrote.

The story, as the researchers tell it, is simple and confusing. She felt her body was stable. But her results stayed abnormal, so the doctors kept or increased the treatment. We do not say here which test led the doctors’ decision. This frustrated her and made her feel like a stranger. Maybe the heaviest part is feeling well and being told by a page that you are not.

Some might read this as a complaint about doctors. The researchers read it more carefully: the report was not neutral information. It was an example of what they call “indicator-centered misrecognition”, where lab numbers overpowered what she feels in her own body. It is as if the paper spoke about her louder than she could.

The researchers also stress something important: the women did not reject medical follow-up or tests. Xiao-mian was not asking to ignore the numbers. She asked that her knowledge of her own body be heard next to them, not instead of them.

If your feeling about your body differs from a test result, you may know this feeling of being a stranger, and you are not alone in it.

A small step may be to write one sentence in your own voice before your visit: “I feel this, my result is this, and I want to talk about this.” Then bring it to your doctor. Do not stop or change a medicine yourself because of how you feel; that decision is made with your doctor. This is not medical advice.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): The ANA test usually stays positive in most people with lupus, often for life, and it is not used alone to measure disease activity. Activity is followed with other tests, urine tests and symptoms. Kidney lupus and other problems can be silent with no symptoms, so feeling well is important but does not rule out active disease. Ask your doctor which of your tests are followed and what they mean for you. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

Accountant, South Africa: “I just go on”Chartered accountant · 15+ years in the same role
  • Chartered accountant
  • 15+ years in the same role

More than fifteen years in one job, making numbers match. But what happened in her body had no line in any budget.

She is a chartered accountant in South Africa, and she lives with lupus. Lupus is an autoimmune disease: the immune system attacks the body’s own tissues. She hid her diagnosis for most of her career. When she described how she gets through her days, she said: “I just go on.” About pain, she said: “If my knees hurt, I do not complain, I’m just quiet.”

The article does not say what was inside her at those moments. But the word “quiet” opens a door to imagination. Maybe the silence was sometimes heavier than the pain. Maybe each morning she made a small calculation: how much can I carry today before someone notices? This is our imagination, not the article.

The two researchers read her choices as a pull between three things: doing more than is asked, caring for her child, and fearing she would be seen as unfit for work just because she needs care. They write that she disclosed her illness only after she had proven her work ethic.

So the silence that began as armor was not the end of the story. A day came when she told. It is as if telling needed a stock of proof first. We do not know how she felt then; the article does not say.

Maybe you know this quiet calculation, and you are not alone.

A small step may be to choose one person you trust, at home or at work, and tell them one sentence about how you are. This does not mean telling everyone; the decision to disclose at work is yours. This is not medical advice.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Joint pain is common in lupus. It is worth telling your doctor rather than hiding it, because it can mean disease activity that can be treated, or it may have another cause. Contact your team if the pain increases, a joint swells, or it comes with fever or unusual tiredness. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

It is as if she keeps a long list: who needs leave, who needs an explanation, who needs to be heard. And at the bottom of the list there is one name that asks for nothing.

She is a human resources manager in South Africa, and she lives with lupus. Lupus is an autoimmune disease: the immune system attacks the body’s own tissues. Maybe her work is built on caring about other people’s needs. The article does not say how she ran her days, but it gives one short sentence from her that is enough: “I’m actually last on the list.”

Then she told her manager about her illness, and her manager’s response was kind.

The researchers see a turning point in her story: a gradual letting go of guilt, and a realization that being kind to yourself is also a form of leadership. This is the researchers’ wording, not hers.

Maybe the guilt was not about anything she did, but about needing. Maybe it whispered that her rest takes time from others. This is our imagination; the article does not describe what was inside her.

Maybe you also know what it is to put yourself last on the list, and you are not alone.

A small step may be to write yourself one line higher on the list, even for one day: a doctor visit, an hour of rest, or one sentence to someone you trust about how you are. This is not medical advice.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Tiredness is one of the most common complaints in lupus, and it has several causes, some of them treatable, such as disease activity, anemia (low hemoglobin, the blood protein that carries oxygen), poor sleep, or depression. Tell your doctor; do not see it as weak will. Rest and regular appointments are part of care, not a luxury. Do not stop or lower a medicine on your own, however busy your day is. Contact your team quickly if tiredness suddenly gets worse, or comes with fever, shortness of breath, or swelling of the feet or face. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.

A woman in Rome: “I wasn’t crazy”Age 34 · 2 years with lupus
  • Age 34
  • 2 years with lupus

A name that came late, and brought her relief. Then a pandemic came, and the same name became a reason to be afraid.

She is a 34-year-old woman who works in beauty care. She took part in a study by the lupus clinic at Sapienza University in Rome. The study collected stories that patients wrote themselves, before and after the COVID-19 lockdown (the period when the coronavirus pandemic closed daily life). This is called “narrative medicine”: the patient writes their story so the medical team can understand how it is to live with the illness. She had lived with lupus for two years. Lupus is an autoimmune disease: the immune system attacks the body’s own tissues.

About the moment of diagnosis she wrote: “At first, I felt relief when I learned I had a disease... I wasn’t crazy.”

Maybe this sentence came after months of symptoms that nobody could name, until she began to doubt herself. This is our imagination; the article does not say how her journey began.

Then the lockdown came. After it she wrote: “If I get infected, I don’t know if I would be so strong... I’m afraid... for me and my family.”

The study’s own title asks how the perception of disease changes through narrative medicine. In this story there is a small answer: the word that saved her from doubting herself did not protect her from fear for herself and her family. It is as if relief and fear live in the same heart at two different times.

Maybe you also know what it is to have a name for your illness that brings relief, and then something frightening comes anyway. You are not alone.

A small step may be to tell your medical team one sentence about your fear, not only your symptoms: “This is what worries me.” Fears are part of what you can be asked about. This is not medical advice.

Medical point (general information, not from the article): Some lupus medicines lower the immune response, which may raise the chance of infection, but this differs between people, depending on the medicine, the dose and the disease activity. Ask your doctor which vaccines suit you and how to protect yourself. Do not stop a medicine out of fear of infection, because stopping it can set off a flare. Contact your team if you have fever, cough or shortness of breath. Constant worry or sadness is also worth telling your team; emotional support is part of care. It does not replace your doctor’s advice.
