Understanding treatment

Understand what your treatment is trying to do for you.

You do not need to memorize every lupus medicine. Start with your own plan: why you take each treatment, what benefit you and your healthcare team are looking for, what needs monitoring, and what matters to you in daily life.

Try the two medication questions ↓

Three things to remember

Lupus treatment is individualized. Feeling better is important, but symptoms are not the only way treatment is assessed. Do not start, stop or change a medicine based on general website information.

Safety first

Do not stop or change prescribed treatment on your own. Seek medical assessment for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms. View relevant guidance

Medication explained with an everyday example

Hydroxychloroquine

Main idea: this medicine can help calm some immune responses involved in inflammation. It may reduce lupus activity and flares; it is not a fast pain reliever or a cure.

Imagine an overly sensitive alarm system

An alarm may go off repeatedly even when there is no fire. In lupus, the immune system can react to healthy tissue and trigger inflammatory signals.

Think of hydroxychloroquine as helping turn down some of those extra signals, not switching off the entire immune system.

Where the analogy ends: the medicine cannot perfectly tell useful immune signals from harmful ones, and its exact action in autoimmune diseases remains incompletely understood.

What benefit might I notice?
It may help some skin and joint symptoms and reduce lupus flares. Responses vary.
When might it help?
It may begin working after several weeks, often in one or two months. Full benefit can take up to six months.
How should I take it?
Follow your prescription. Taking tablets with food or milk may reduce stomach upset. Do not stop or alter the dose on your own.
What should be monitored?
Agree an eye-screening plan based on duration, dose and risk factors. Report any new vision changes promptly.
How does it work in the body? Read more

It is thought to affect communication between certain immune cells and inflammatory signals. Its exact mechanism in lupus is not fully established. Inform your pharmacist or doctor about all medicines, including antacids and supplements, because of possible interactions.

Medicine explained step by step

Corticosteroids (such as prednisone)

What it does: Can rapidly reduce inflammation during lupus flares.

An everyday analogy

Think of emergency brakes slowing a vehicle: useful when needed, but the dose should be eased only with a safe plan.

Analogy limits: The brakes analogy does not represent effects on the body's natural hormone system.

How it works
They affect several immune and inflammatory pathways.
Expected benefit
Effects may appear quickly; your condition determines duration and dose.
Safety and follow-up
Do not stop suddenly on your own. Discuss blood pressure, glucose, bone and infection monitoring.

Medicine explained step by step

NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen)

What it does: May relieve some pain and swelling, but do not control underlying lupus activity on their own.

An everyday analogy

Like lowering the volume of a pain alarm: the signal may ease even though the underlying problem remains.

Analogy limits: A quieter pain signal does not prove organ inflammation has stopped.

How it works
They reduce prostaglandin production involved in pain and inflammation.
Expected benefit
Pain relief may begin within hours; responses vary.
Safety and follow-up
They may cause stomach bleeding and affect kidneys, blood pressure and the heart; check before use if you have kidney involvement.

Medicine explained step by step

Azathioprine

What it does: May help control immune activity and reduce steroid needs in selected cases.

An everyday analogy

Imagine slowing a workshop producing too many responders to a false alarm.

Analogy limits: It can affect healthy blood cells as well, so monitoring matters.

How it works
It limits building blocks needed for division of some immune cells.
Expected benefit
The benefit develops over weeks to months, depending on the goal.
Safety and follow-up
Blood counts and liver tests are essential. Ask about TPMT testing. Mention gout medicines such as allopurinol or febuxostat and report fever or bruising.

Medicine explained step by step

Methotrexate

What it does: Can treat selected joint and skin manifestations of lupus.

An everyday analogy

Imagine slowing production lines contributing to inflammation; improvement takes time.

Analogy limits: Rheumatology dosing must not be confused with regimens used for other conditions.

How it works
At rheumatology doses it changes several cellular and inflammatory processes.
Expected benefit
Improvement may take several weeks; full response takes longer.
Safety and follow-up
Usually taken ONCE A WEEK for rheumatology, not daily. Blood and liver tests are needed; folic acid may be prescribed. It is unsafe in pregnancy.

Medicine explained step by step

Mycophenolate

What it does: Can reduce immune activity harming organs, including in some cases of lupus nephritis.

An everyday analogy

Imagine slowing repeated orders to make additional immune cells.

Analogy limits: Its effects are not restricted to harmful immune cells.

How it works
It blocks a pathway some immune cells use to make components needed for division.
Expected benefit
Clinicians follow symptoms, blood and urine tests and kidney function when relevant.
Safety and follow-up
Infection and blood count risks need monitoring. It can cause miscarriage and severe birth defects. Discuss pregnancy planning or suspected pregnancy urgently.

Medicine explained step by step

Cyclophosphamide

What it does: May be used for severe, organ-threatening lupus under specialist care.

An everyday analogy

Imagine a stronger emergency response to dangerous immune activity, requiring closer safety checks.

Analogy limits: A stronger response also carries significant risks and is not right for everyone.

How it works
It affects genetic material in dividing cells, including immune cells.
Expected benefit
Specialist protocols define dosing, duration and response checks.
Safety and follow-up
Risks include infection, blood count changes, bladder injury and fertility or pregnancy harm. Discuss fertility preservation and report fever or blood in urine promptly.

Medicine explained step by step

Belimumab

What it does: A targeted biologic added in selected cases of lupus, including some kidney disease.

An everyday analogy

Imagine reducing a support signal that helps some overactive B cells persist.

Analogy limits: It does not remove every B cell or work immediately for everyone.

How it works
It blocks BLyS/BAFF, a protein supporting survival of some B cells.
Expected benefit
Benefit can take months; the team checks agreed treatment goals.
Safety and follow-up
Watch for infection or infusion/injection reactions. Promptly report major mood changes or thoughts of self-harm.
Check your understanding — choose an answer
You develop fever or significant mood changes on belimumab. What is best?

Medicine explained step by step

Anifrolumab

What it does: A biologic option for selected adults with active lupus.

An everyday analogy

Imagine blocking receipt of one repeating immune alert, not shutting down all signals.

Analogy limits: It does not switch off every immune pathway.

How it works
It blocks the type I interferon receptor pathway.
Expected benefit
Response is reviewed over follow-up visits for the outcomes treated.
Safety and follow-up
May increase infection risk, including shingles. Discuss vaccination and report fever or a painful blistering rash.

Know what not to manage alone.

Do not stop or change lupus medicines on your own, particularly corticosteroids or immune-modifying treatment. If you develop a possible side effect, infection, pregnancy, or another important health change, ask your healthcare team or pharmacist what to do.
