Understanding treatment

Understand what your treatment is trying to do for you.

You do not need to memorize every lupus medicine. Start with your own plan: why you take each treatment, what benefit you and your healthcare team are looking for, what needs monitoring, and what matters to you in daily life.

Try five real-life teach-back cases ↓

Three things to remember

Lupus treatment is individualized. Feeling better is important, but symptoms are not the only way treatment is assessed. Do not start, stop or change a medicine based on general website information.

Safety first

Do not stop or change prescribed treatment on your own. Seek medical assessment for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms. View relevant guidance

Compare medicines by their role — detailed guide

Immunosuppressant broadly means a medicine that reduces part of the immune system's activity. One drug may limit the multiplication of certain cells; another may block one particular inflammatory signal. Similar labels do not mean identical medicines.

Important: Sometimes clinicians intentionally prescribe several medicines together, especially for lupus kidney disease. Do not combine or stop treatments based on similarity alone. Share your full medicine and supplement list with your clinician and pharmacist.

Group 1

1. Long-term foundation treatment

One shared analogy: Imagine an overly sensitive alarm system. Hydroxychloroquine may quiet some excessive immune signals. It does not switch off all immunity, and its exact mechanism remains incompletely understood.

Hydroxychloroquine

Why it may be used
Often forms part of long-term lupus care to reduce flares and some skin or joint symptoms; benefits usually take longer than a few days.
What makes it different?
A common foundation medicine, not a substitute for treatment of severe organ inflammation.
Safety and key interactions
Arrange eye screening. Separate oral antacids from hydroxychloroquine by about four hours. Ask about interactions with medicines affecting heart rhythm.

Group 2

2. Fast inflammation control versus symptom relief

One shared analogy: Imagine a warning light on a moving vehicle. Steroids can act like emergency brakes for important inflammation, while NSAIDs can turn down the noise of pain. Neither role is identical to maintaining long-term disease control.

Corticosteroids (prednisone)

Why it may be used
Reduce active inflammation relatively quickly; often used temporarily while other therapies begin working.
What makes it different?
Broader anti-inflammatory effects, but long-term or high-dose exposure brings important harms.
Safety and key interactions
Never stop abruptly on your own. Adding ibuprofen or naproxen can increase stomach bleeding risk; check before combining.

Open full NA7N medicine guide (+/−) → Original source [1, 2, 5]

NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen)

Why it may be used
Can help selected patients with pain or mild inflammation, but do not replace treatment for lupus kidney or other organ disease.
What makes it different?
Act on pain/inflammation pathways rather than targeting underlying lupus immune activity.
Safety and key interactions
Risk of stomach bleeding, kidney injury and heart effects. Take care with blood thinners, steroids and other NSAIDs.

Group 3

3. Medicines that limit immune-cell multiplication — one shared analogy

One shared analogy: Think of a factory making many immune-system defenders. In lupus some of these can cause harm. The four medicines below can reduce the production or activity of certain cells, but through different mechanisms and with different monitoring needs.

Azathioprine

Why it may be used
Can help control disease and reduce steroid exposure in selected longer-term plans.
What makes it different?
Slows cell multiplication; may be used for maintenance in some situations, but is not interchangeable with mycophenolate for every kidney problem.
Safety and key interactions
Needs blood and liver monitoring. Gout medicines such as allopurinol and febuxostat can create severe interactions requiring specialist avoidance or adjustment.

Open full NA7N medicine guide (+/−) → Original source [1, 5]

Methotrexate

Why it may be used
May help some lupus joint and skin manifestations after several weeks.
What makes it different?
A key practical distinction: rheumatology dosing is usually ONCE WEEKLY, not daily.
Safety and key interactions
Daily rather than weekly use can cause severe toxicity. Trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole may dangerously affect blood cells when combined. Discuss pregnancy, liver tests and other medicines.

Open full NA7N medicine guide (+/−) → Original source [1, 5, 6]

Mycophenolate

Why it may be used
A key part of several treatment strategies for lupus kidney inflammation.
What makes it different?
Limits building blocks needed for immune-cell multiplication; kidney response is followed with urine and kidney blood tests.
Safety and key interactions
Carries major miscarriage and birth-defect risks; discuss pregnancy ahead of time. Some antacids affect absorption; ask about spacing doses. Different formulations are not freely interchangeable.

Open full NA7N medicine guide (+/−) → Original source [1, 3, 5]

Cyclophosphamide

Why it may be used
Used by specialists in selected severe or organ-threatening lupus.
What makes it different?
A more intensive effect on dividing cells, with closely monitored protocols; not best for everyone.
Safety and key interactions
Can affect blood counts, bladder and fertility, and harms pregnancy. Discuss fertility preservation, and report fever or blood in urine promptly.

Group 4

4. Biologics: different messages in the immune communication system

One shared analogy: Imagine a communications room sending signals to immune defenders. Both medicines block particular signals, but they target different pathways. Neither is automatically stronger or better for everyone.

Belimumab

Why it may be used
Can be added for certain lupus cases and some lupus kidney disease.
What makes it different?
Reduces a survival signal for some B cells, which are immune cells involved in making antibodies.
Safety and key interactions
Check for infection and injection/infusion reactions, and report significant mood changes. Do not combine biologics yourself.

Open full NA7N medicine guide (+/−) → Original source [1, 3, 5]

Anifrolumab

Why it may be used
May help selected adults with active lupus in specialist care.
What makes it different?
Blocks reception of one inflammatory message called type I interferon, rather than the B-cell survival signal.
Safety and key interactions
Review infection and vaccine risks, especially shingles or painful blistering rashes. Do not combine biologics yourself.

Know what not to manage alone.

Do not stop or change lupus medicines on your own, particularly corticosteroids or immune-modifying treatment. If you develop a possible side effect, infection, pregnancy, or another important health change, ask your healthcare team or pharmacist what to do. Five real-life cases: explain what you understood

This is not a memory test or medical assessment. Read each scenario, explain your reasoning in your own words, then open the review points. Your answer is not sent or stored.

Case 1 / 5

Two medicines in one plan

Someone taking hydroxychloroquine is prescribed mycophenolate for lupus nephritis. They think both suppress immunity and want to stop one. How would you explain the difference and the need for the prescribed plan?

Show review points after answering

Hydroxychloroquine often supports long-term lupus control, while mycophenolate is used for particular immune-cell activity and kidney disease. The combination can be intentional; do not stop either without the specialist.

If this is unclear, return to the medicine comparison and explain it once more.

Case 2 / 5

A weekly prescription

A patient sees methotrexate 'every Sunday' but thinks it means 'every day'. Explain the danger and the safe next action.

Show review points after answering

Rheumatology methotrexate is generally weekly; accidentally taking it daily can cause serious harm. Confirm the label with a pharmacist before taking it, and seek urgent advice after a dosing mistake.

If this is unclear, return to the medicine comparison and explain it once more.

Case 3 / 5

An over-the-counter painkiller

A person on steroids with kidney involvement wants ibuprofen. Explain why it might help pain but may still be risky.

Show review points after answering

NSAIDs may relieve pain but can harm kidneys and increase stomach bleeding, particularly with steroids. Ask a clinician or pharmacist for a safer plan.

If this is unclear, return to the medicine comparison and explain it once more.

Case 4 / 5

Adding a new prescription

A person on azathioprine is offered gout medicine, or someone on methotrexate receives an antibiotic. Explain what they must tell their clinicians.

Show review points after answering

Give the full medicine list and schedule before starting. Some gout medicines interact seriously with azathioprine; trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole can interact dangerously with methotrexate.

If this is unclear, return to the medicine comparison and explain it once more.

Case 5 / 5

Planning pregnancy

Someone taking mycophenolate wants to become pregnant and assumes switching independently to a friend's biologic medicine is safer. What would you explain?

Show review points after answering

Mycophenolate poses serious pregnancy risks; the specialist team must plan alternative treatment, timing and disease control. Do not self-switch therapies.

If this is unclear, return to the medicine comparison and explain it once more.
