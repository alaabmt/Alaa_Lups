Understand lupus

Lupus does not look the same for everyone.

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease. It can affect different parts of the body, and its pattern and severity vary from person to person and may change over time.

Important

This information is educational. It does not diagnose your condition or replace advice from your healthcare team.

Key information

What is lupus?

The immune system normally protects the body from infection and disease. In lupus, the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues, which can cause inflammation and sometimes tissue damage. Lupus may affect the skin, joints, kidneys, blood cells, heart, lungs or nervous system. Having lupus does not mean all of these organs will be affected.

Patient guide

Why does lupus happen?

There is no single known cause. Current evidence points to an interaction between genetic, immune, hormonal and environmental factors. Lupus is not contagious.

Patient guide

How is lupus diagnosed?

There is no single test that diagnoses lupus by itself. Clinicians combine your medical history, symptoms, examination, blood and urine tests, and sometimes additional tests or a biopsy. A positive antinuclear antibody (ANA) test alone does not confirm lupus.

Patient guide

Flares and quieter periods

Lupus can have periods when inflammation or symptoms increase and periods when disease is quieter. Fatigue alone or one abnormal test does not prove a flare. Your clinician considers symptoms, examination, tests, affected organs and your usual disease pattern.

Patient guide

Why follow-up matters

Some problems can appear on tests before they cause obvious symptoms. Kidney involvement is an important example, so regular monitoring may be needed even when you feel well.
